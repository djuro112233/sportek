# Sexually transmitted infections: prevention and testing basics

## What STIs are

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are infections passed from one person to another mainly through sexual contact. Common ones include chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, trichomoniasis, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B and HIV. Many are curable and all can be treated or managed.

## Many STIs have no symptoms

- Most people with chlamydia or gonorrhoea, and many with HIV in the early phase, have no symptoms at all. You cannot tell by looking whether someone has an STI.
- Possible symptoms include unusual discharge from the vagina or penis, burning when passing urine, sores, blisters or warts on the genitals, lower belly pain, pain during sex, or bleeding between periods.
- Untreated STIs can cause infertility, chronic pain, problems in pregnancy, and increase the risk of getting HIV.

## Prevention

- Condoms (male or female), used correctly every time, are the most effective way to prevent most STIs, including HIV.
- Vaccines protect against HPV and hepatitis B; the HPV vaccine is recommended for girls and boys from the age of nine to fourteen and can also be given later.
- Having fewer partners and talking openly with partners about testing reduces risk.
- Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a medicine that people at high risk of HIV can take to prevent infection; a clinician can advise whether it is suitable.

## Testing

- Testing is the only way to know. Ask for a test if you have had unprotected sex, a new partner, symptoms, or if a partner has an STI.
- Tests are simple: a urine sample, a swab or a blood test. Some results are available the same day; HIV self-tests are available in some places.
- Pregnant women are tested for HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B at the first antenatal visit so the baby can be protected.
- Testing is confidential. Adolescents have the right to testing and treatment.

## If a test is positive

- Most STIs are cured with a course of treatment; HIV and herpes can be controlled with ongoing treatment so that people live long, healthy lives.
- Recent partners need to be informed and tested so they can be treated; a clinic can help you do this anonymously.
- Avoid sex until treatment is completed and the clinician says it is safe.
- Finish the full treatment even if symptoms disappear.

## Where to go

- Primary health care centres, gynaecologists, dermatovenerology clinics, youth-friendly centres and public health institutes offer testing and treatment.
- Emergency departments and sexual violence services can provide protective treatment after an assault.
