# Perinatal mental health: baby blues and when to seek help

## Feelings during and after pregnancy

Pregnancy and the first year after birth bring big changes in hormones, sleep, relationships and identity. Strong feelings are normal. Many women experience worry, sadness or irritability at some point; mental health problems in this period are common and treatable, and they are nobody's fault.

## The baby blues

- Around the third to fifth day after birth, many mothers feel tearful, anxious, irritable or overwhelmed.
- The "baby blues" are linked to hormone changes, tiredness and the intensity of the first days.
- They usually pass on their own within about two weeks, with rest, food, sleep whenever possible and support from family and friends.
- No treatment is needed for the baby blues, but it is good to tell your midwife or nurse how you feel.

## When it is more than the blues

Seek help from a health worker if:

- Low mood, loss of interest or constant anxiety last more than two weeks, or start weeks or months after birth.
- You cannot sleep even when the baby sleeps, or you cannot eat.
- You feel hopeless, worthless or guilty most of the time.
- You feel unable to care for the baby, or have no feelings for the baby and this worries you.
- Panic attacks or frightening repeated thoughts interfere with daily life.
- These can be signs of perinatal depression or anxiety, which affect about one in five women in low- and middle-income settings and respond well to treatment.

## Seek help immediately if

- You have thoughts of harming yourself or the baby.
- You hear or see things others do not, feel confused, or believe things that others say are not real; this can be postpartum psychosis, a rare emergency that needs urgent care.
- Someone around you says they are very worried about you.

Call emergency services or a helpline, or go to the nearest emergency department. Ask someone to stay with you.

## What helps

- Talking about how you feel with a partner, family, friends or other mothers.
- Sleep and rest whenever possible; sharing night care.
- Gentle physical activity and time outdoors.
- Psychological support (counselling or talking therapy) is the first-line treatment for mild and moderate depression and anxiety; it is effective and safe in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
- When medicines are needed, many are compatible with pregnancy and breastfeeding; a doctor will choose with you.

## For partners and family

- Fathers and partners can also experience depression and anxiety in this period.
- Practical help, listening without judging and encouraging the mother to seek care make a real difference.
- Pregnancy and the postnatal period are also when violence at home can begin or worsen; help is available and confidential.
