# Breastfeeding basics

## Why breastfeeding is recommended

Breast milk gives a baby everything it needs for the first six months. It protects against infections, is always the right temperature and costs nothing. WHO recommends starting breastfeeding within the first hour after birth, exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, and continued breastfeeding with other foods up to two years or beyond.

## Getting started

- Put the baby skin to skin on your chest right after birth; most babies find the breast on their own within the first hour.
- The first milk, colostrum, is thick and yellowish and comes in small amounts; it is exactly what a newborn needs.
- Fuller milk usually "comes in" around the third or fourth day.
- Feed on demand: whenever the baby shows signs of hunger (rooting, hand to mouth, sucking), usually eight to twelve times in 24 hours in the early weeks.
- Let the baby finish one breast before offering the other.

## Positioning and attachment

- Hold the baby close, facing you, with head and body in a straight line.
- Bring the baby to the breast with the nose at the level of the nipple; wait for a wide open mouth.
- A good latch: more of the dark area is visible above the baby's top lip than below, the chin touches the breast, the lower lip is turned out and sucking is slow and deep with swallowing.
- Breastfeeding should not hurt once the baby is attached; pain usually means the attachment needs to be corrected.

## Signs that the baby is feeding well

- At least six wet nappies a day after day five, with pale urine.
- Regular soft yellow stools in the first weeks.
- The baby is content after most feeds and is gaining weight after the first week.
- You can hear swallowing during feeds.

## Common difficulties

- Sore or cracked nipples: check the attachment; a drop of breast milk on the nipple can help healing.
- Engorgement (full, hard breasts): feed often, express a little milk to soften the breast, apply cold compresses between feeds.
- A tender, red, hot area on the breast with fever can be mastitis; keep feeding and seek care.
- Worries about "not enough milk" are very common; frequent feeding increases supply. Ask for support before giving formula.

## Good to know

- Exclusive breastfeeding means no water, tea, juice or other foods for the first six months.
- Most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding, but check with a clinician or pharmacist before taking any.
- Mothers living with HIV can breastfeed safely while taking their treatment; a clinician will advise.
- Expressed breast milk can be stored and given by cup when the mother is away.
- Help is available from midwives, nurses, paediatricians and peer support groups.
