# Antenatal care: visits during pregnancy

## Why antenatal care matters

Antenatal care is the care you receive from health workers during pregnancy. It helps keep you and your baby healthy, finds problems early, and prepares you for birth and life with a newborn. The World Health Organization recommends at least eight contacts with a health worker during pregnancy, starting in the first twelve weeks.

## When to go

- Book your first visit as soon as you know you are pregnant, ideally before twelve weeks.
- A typical schedule has one contact in the first trimester, two in the second trimester (around 20 and 26 weeks) and five in the third trimester (around 30, 34, 36, 38 and 40 weeks).
- Go to extra visits whenever you have a concern or a danger sign; you do not need to wait for the next appointment.

## What happens at the first visit

- The health worker asks about your health history, previous pregnancies, medicines you take and your living situation, and listens to your worries.
- Your blood pressure, weight and height are measured.
- Blood tests check for anaemia, blood group, HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B, so that any infection can be treated and the baby protected.
- A urine test checks for infection and protein.
- An ultrasound scan before 24 weeks is recommended to confirm the due date, see whether there is more than one baby, and check the placenta.

## What happens at follow-up visits

- Blood pressure is measured at every visit; a rise can be an early sign of pre-eclampsia.
- The health worker measures your belly and listens to the baby's heartbeat.
- You are asked about the baby's movements, bleeding, pain, headaches, swelling and how you feel emotionally.
- Supplements and vaccinations (such as tetanus) are discussed and given according to the schedule.
- Later visits cover the birth plan, danger signs, breastfeeding and family planning after birth.

## Your rights at the visit

- You can ask questions and ask for explanations in plain language; you can bring a partner, relative or friend.
- You have the right to respectful, confidential care, whatever your age or marital status.
- If something feels wrong or you are treated badly, you can ask for another health worker.

## Preparing for a visit

- Bring your health booklet or previous results.
- Write down your questions and the medicines or supplements you take.
- Note the date of your last period; it helps estimate the due date.
- Ask who to call and where to go if a problem appears between visits.
