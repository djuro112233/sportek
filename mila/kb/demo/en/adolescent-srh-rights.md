# Adolescents: sexual and reproductive health, rights and consent

## Your body, your rights

- Adolescents have the right to accurate information about their bodies, sexuality, contraception and infections, and to confidential, respectful health services.
- You can ask questions at a health centre, a youth-friendly clinic or a school nurse without being judged.
- Health workers should keep what you tell them confidential; the exception is when you or someone else is in serious danger, and even then they should explain what they will do.
- Nobody can force you into sex, marriage or pregnancy. Child marriage and forced marriage are violations of your rights.

## Consent

- Consent means freely and clearly agreeing to sexual activity. It must be given every time, can be withdrawn at any moment, and cannot be given by someone who is asleep, drunk, drugged, pressured or afraid.
- Silence or not saying no is not consent.
- In Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Serbia and Croatia the law sets an age of consent, and sexual acts by an adult with a child below that age are a crime, whatever the child says.
- Sex with someone who has authority over you (a teacher, coach, relative, employer) is a particular risk; it is never your fault.

## Puberty and change

- Puberty brings body changes, periods, erections, acne, sweating and strong feelings; the timing varies a lot and all of it is normal.
- Attraction to others, and uncertainty about it, is a normal part of growing up.
- Masturbation is normal and harmless.

## Preventing pregnancy and infections

- Condoms protect against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections and are available without prescription.
- Adolescents can use any contraceptive method, including long-acting methods; age alone is not a reason to refuse a method.
- Emergency contraception is available after unprotected sex, and works best the sooner it is used.
- The HPV vaccine is recommended for girls and boys from the age of nine to fourteen.

## Pregnancy in adolescence

- Girls who become pregnant have the right to antenatal care, to continue school and to support. Early antenatal care is especially important because pregnancy at a young age carries more health risks.
- Options and the law on abortion should be explained by a health worker in a confidential conversation.

## If something is wrong

- If someone has touched you, pressured you, shared your images or hurt you, you can tell a trusted adult, a school counsellor, a health worker, the police or a child helpline. It is not your fault and you will not be in trouble for asking for help.
- Online pressure to send intimate pictures is a form of abuse; you can refuse and report it.
- You can bring a friend to an appointment, and you can ask the health worker to speak with you alone.
