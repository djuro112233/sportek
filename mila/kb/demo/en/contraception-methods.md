# Contraception methods: an overview

Contraception means preventing pregnancy. There are many safe methods, and almost every healthy person can use most of them. A trained provider can help you choose a method that fits your life, your health and your plans. You can change methods at any time.

## How the main methods work

- Long-acting reversible methods: the intrauterine device (IUD, "coil") and the contraceptive implant. A provider places them; they protect for several years and fertility returns quickly after removal. They are among the most effective methods.
- Hormonal methods you take or receive on a schedule: the pill (taken daily), the injection (given every few months) and the patch or vaginal ring (changed on a regular schedule). They prevent ovulation or thicken the mucus of the cervix.
- Barrier methods: male and female condoms stop sperm from reaching the egg. Condoms are the only method that also protects against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV.
- Fertility awareness methods: tracking the menstrual cycle to avoid unprotected sex on fertile days. They need regular cycles, training and a cooperating partner, and they are less effective than other methods.
- Lactational amenorrhoea method (LAM): exclusive breastfeeding during the first six months after birth, while periods have not returned, can be used as temporary contraception.
- Permanent methods: female sterilisation and vasectomy are for people who are sure they do not want children in the future.
- Emergency contraception can be used after unprotected sex; it is a back-up, not a regular method.

## How effective are they?

- Implants, IUDs and sterilisation are the most effective: fewer than one in a hundred users become pregnant in a year.
- The pill, injection, patch and ring are very effective when used exactly as instructed; with typical use some pregnancies happen because of missed pills or late injections.
- Condoms are effective when used correctly every time; typical use is less effective.
- Withdrawal and fertility awareness are the least reliable methods.

## Choosing a method

- Think about how often you want to deal with the method: daily, monthly, every few months, or every few years.
- Tell the provider about any health conditions, medicines you take, smoking and whether you are breastfeeding; some methods are not advised in some situations.
- Using a condom together with another method gives protection against both pregnancy and STIs ("dual protection").
- Most methods do not affect future fertility; after stopping, pregnancy is possible again, often within weeks.

## Side effects and worries

- Changes in bleeding pattern are the most common side effect of hormonal methods and the hormonal IUD, and are usually not dangerous.
- Hormonal contraception does not cause infertility and the pill does not need "breaks".
- If a side effect bothers you, talk to a provider; a different method may suit you better.

## Where to get contraception

- Primary health care centres (dom zdravlja), gynaecologists and family planning counselling centres provide information and methods.
- Condoms are sold in pharmacies and shops without a prescription.
- Your questions and your choice are confidential. Young people have the right to information and services too.
